Enshi, Zhangjiajie & Fenghuang

While I’ve been in China I’ve been reading other people’s online posts about where they’ve visited and that’s been helpful in determining destinations. I read about a less-visited city called Enshi in Hubei Province and decided to go there, and it turned out to be one of the highlights of my time in China, mostly because some locals decided to show me a good time, although the very green and mountainous scenery was wonderful, too.

Enshi is the capital of the Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, and was cool because it isn’t really developed for tourism like the other places I went were. It’s just like, a normal city. Cities in China are often referred to by tiers, so for example, Beijing and Shanghai are considered Tier 1, which means they’ve got the largest and most developed economies and infrastructure and largest populations. But this tier system is not official, it’s just something investors, analysts and other observers have come up with to rank cities’ economic/social status. Enshi is considered a Tier 5 city, which is the lowest ranking, but that just means it’s smaller and less developed.

In fact it’s beautiful and really interesting, here’s a view from the top of the wall surrounding an ancient palace/temple complex:

The sights to see in Enshi mostly emphasize the local Tujia and Miao cultures’ heritage. During all the various dynasties — up to the early 20th century, these and other ethnic groups were woven into the fabric of the imperial ruling system by a delegation of power, called the tusi system. Through this system, the local leader, called a tusi, could collect taxes that went back to the emperor and rule their community fairly independently. Enshi was home to a Tujia tusi, and the complex that the video above was filmed at was the tusi’s home over hundreds of years. I believe the complex has been mostly rebuilt and is not original, but it’s a cool window into this history. There are lots of paintings and carvings and sculptures of the White Tiger all around the site. The White Tiger was an important symbol to the Ba people, which the Tujia people are considered to have descended from.

One night I went out to eat near my hotel, which for some unknown reason had decorated the room I was in with a giant Los Angeles skyline poster (pic below). A married couple was operating the restaurant I went to, and the husband and I were chatting and then he asked if I wanted to hang out. Actually he asked me if I wanted to drink corn liquor that night, lol, but I had been out all day and was not up for it. So we settled on dinner the next evening.

I went out and did some stuff the following day and the guy messaged me, asking me if I wanted to go swimming in the river that runs through the city before dinner. I said yes, of course, and met him and a few of his friends who are regular river swimmers. Nobody spoke English and I have no Chinese language skills, but they were super nice to me, and we communicated via translation apps. We swam in the river and then jumped off this ledge thing a bunch of times. It was super fun.

Then the one dude took me to his sister’s tea shop and they treated me to a tea tasting of the green and black teas they grow in the Enshi area. It was so good.

Then a bunch more people all met us at this nice restaurant and again, they treated me to a huge feast. It was delicious and fun. The pitcher you see on the table is the corn liquor. I don’t need to say more than that.

It ended up being so fun, and like I said, the people were just super nice to me. I got very lucky! These were all experiences I would not have had if it weren’t for these people’s friendliness. I wish I could put up the full pictures with their faces but I don’t know if they’d be cool with that, but you can at least see the thumbs ups.

Enshi is also within easy driving distance of some really cool stuff, including the Enshi Grand Canyon, also called the Yunlong Ground Fissure, where you could visit both the bottom of the canyon and an area called Qixingzhai, aka Seven Star Village, in the mountains up above. There is also the Suobuya Stone Forest, which was apparently formed in the Ordovician Period, about 460 million years ago.

Fenghuang

And then I spent a couple of days in Fenghuang. The Tuojiang River runs through the ancient part of town, which has been there since the very early 1700s. It’s a huge tourist trap, but it’s fun. The nightlife is kind of the draw, everything lights up and there are live music bars everywhere and lots and lots of street food vendors. It’s a lot, but it’s fun to be a part of it.

Fenghuang is the word for a mythological bird that seems to often be translated to “phoenix,” but as I understand, it doesn’t have the “rising from the ashes” meaning. It’s more of the king of the birds type thing, it rules over all the other kinds of birds, and symbolizes positive things.

Qiliang Cave is also in Fenghuang, and I was lucky to go there on a day where I saw maybe 5 other tourists there. It’s a really big cave with lots of cool sections. There’s also a petting zoo right out front for some reason.
