    Cambodia November 4–10, 2026

    Phnom Penh

    Phnom Penh is the capital of Cambodia and is located at the convergence of the Mekong, Tonlé Sap and Bassac rivers. The city’s about 90 miles (150 km) from the Gulf of Thailand and about 2.3 million people live there.

    The majority culture and language in Cambodia is Khmer (which locals pronounce “Kuh-my”), although there are many less numerous ethnic groups as well. Khmer people actually call their country Kampuchea.

    I stayed in a Phnom Penh neighborhood not too terribly far from the Royal Palace. Although the city was founded in the 1430s following the fall of Angkor, it didn’t become the permanent capital until 1865, after King Norodom allowed Cambodia to become a French “protectorate,” amid serious threats to its territorial integrity from historical rivals Thailand and Vietnam.

    So, there is a mix of building styles in the city, from the Royal Palace that’s built in the traditional Khmer style to the French colonial buildings that were constructed. The French officially left Cambodia in 1953, and I happened to be in Phnom Penh during Cambodia’s Independence Day festival, which was pretty cool.

    The neighborhood that I stayed in features a lot of small bars, restaurants, shops selling everything from eyeglasses to ice cream, and larger markets where vendors sell fresh produce and meat.

    I started my visit to Cambodia and Phnom Penh by going to the Tuol Sleng Genocide Museum, which is the memorial site of the Khmer Rouge’s S-21 horrific interrogation and detention center. The Khmer Rouge were a communist army and organization that fought against the right wing government that had taken power in Cambodia in a 1970 US-backed coup. The two sides battled in a civil war until 1975, when the Khmer Rouge won and began their brutal four-year reign.

    S-21 was a former high school that was transformed into a prison by the Khmer Rouge after they entered Phnom Penh in 1975. At the prison, people were detained for all manner of alleged crimes, from stealing to being anti-revolutionary.

    According to the information at the museum, many people were innocent. But the Khmer Rouge position reportedly was that, if you were arrested, you were guilty because “Angkar,” the nebulous ruling body, did not make mistakes. Apparently there were also revolutionary slogans, such as, “Better to smash an innocent person than let a guilty person go free.” To “smash” someone meant to kill them.

    Prisoners at S-21 were brutally tortured in order to extract detailed written confessions about their alleged “crimes” and implicate others in their lives. The museum does not spare the visitor from any of the sadistic depravity, and includes graphic photographs, torture implements and human remains (none of which will be shown here).

    For those who want further experience and an opportunity to learn more about the Khmer Rouge years, there is the Choeung Ek Killing Field. It’s a little outside the city and was where prisoners from S-21 were taken to be executed. The brutality continued there and post-Khmer Rouge witnesses found mass graves and evidence of the killing of babies and children. The Khmer Rouge thought it was a waste of resources to use bullets to execute these prisoners, but I’ll spare you the details.

    One thing I didn’t know is that after 1979, when the Khmer Rouge was overthrown by a combination of communist Vietnamese and Cambodian soldiers, the United States, China, the United Kingdom and many other countries continued to recognize the Khmer Rouge as the legitimate government of Cambodia. These countries refused to recognize the new People’s Republic of Kampuchea because it was too close to the communist Vietnamese government.

    There’s a lot more to it but you’ll have to research Cambodian history if you want to know more.

    Of course the country has a long and interesting history that includes a lot more than the Khmer Rouge, some of which is on display at the Royal Palace in Phnom Penh and a couple of museums near it.

    The museums I mentioned were the National Museum and the Sosoro Museum.

    The National Museum houses a lot of older Khmer statuary and stone carvings that date to the Angkor period and reflect the unique mixture of Hinduism and Buddhism that those Khmers adopted as their religion.

    And the Sosoro Museum focuses on the history of the civilization through the use, or non-use, of currency. I got the feeling the exhibit was basically an ad for banks, but there was still a lot of interesting stuff there, haha.
