South Korea July 8–22, 2026

Seoul

After almost three months in China, I flew to Seoul to spend some time and start a visit through the Republic of Korea, aka South Korea. And I have to say, I immediately really liked the city and ended up spending two weeks there. There’s a lot to see and do, it’s in a beautiful area, the food is amazing and it’s just a nice place to spend time. Even though it rained a lot when I was there, I had to really roust myself to get going on the rest of the trip, haha.

I stayed in the Jonggak neighborhood of the Jongno section of town, which has a lot of bars and restaurants and is near a lot of historic sites. Of course the public transportation there is great so anything you want to do is easily accessible via subway and/or bus.

There are a couple of historic palaces, Deoksugung and Gyeonghuigung, right by my hostel.

I went to Deoksugung first, and the grounds are also home to Dondeokjeon Hall, which houses some art exhibits. When I was there, they had one called, “Banhwa: Blossoms of Auspicious Wishes” which featured some cultural items exchanged between France and Korea. Banhwa are tree-shaped objects that were created with precious items like gold and jewels. Apparently the ones on display are replicas because the originals are too fragile to transport from France. But they were recreated by master craftspeople so they look awesome.

Deoksugung grounds

Then right outside Deoksugung there’s a nice neighborhood to walk around in, and as I was doing that, I spotted this really mind bending sculptureof people that are all kind of squashed down. It’s called Jangdokdae and it was created by Yi Hwan-kwon. Jangdokdae is the space where they put jangdok, which are clay pots that are used to store kimchi (which I am eating so much of here) and other fermented food. So the people kind of look like those jars? It’s really disorienting to see it in person, I don’t know if this picture can fully communicate it:

Gyeonghuigung is a bigger palace and is located right at the foot of this mountain, it’s really a beautiful spot. So you’re on the grounds there and it’s all historic buildings and stuff, but you’re also right in the middle of Seoul, so you can see big buildings. Apparently it’s free admission if you dress up in Hanbok, the traditional Korean clothes, so you see tons of people of all backgrounds who’ve rented those outfits walking around (I didn’t, haha).

This is irworobongdo, a throne room motif commonly used during the Joseon era that depicts a sun, moon, streams and five mountain peaks
1 / 6

And of course Seoul is not too far from the demilitarized zone on the border with the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, aka North Korea, so I took a tour to visit that. It was really great, there was a North Korean defector who was on the bus with us up to the DMZ, and she told us her story and answered questions during the ride.

Unfortunately they do not allow photos at most of the places, like at the observation deck where you can see North Korea or in the tunnel we got to tour. They have found four tunnels that extend from the north into the south, and they think there are more that they haven’t found yet, according to the tour guide, a suspicion that’s been reported elsewhere as well.

Seoul is built on the Han River in the midst of many mountains. The Seoul Tower is at the top of one called Namsan, for instance. So I went up there and also hiked up one called Muaksan to a site called the East Beacon Mound, which is where they used to signal to other outlook posts with fires. Here’s the view from Muaksan, and then from Namsan. I have to note, there are so many dragonflies in Seoul, and every other place I’ve been in Korea so far!
