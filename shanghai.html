China April 27–May 2, 2026

Shanghai

I took one of the fastest bullet trains in the world from Beijing to Shanghai. It was hitting around 350 kmph (217 mph) for much of the trip. As long as there’s a good stretch of straightaway, of which there are plenty on that route, the train can do that speed. The route is about 1,320 km long (820 miles) and took about 4.5 hours. Italy, Spain, France, Germany and of course Japan all have steel-wheel bullet trains that can do between 320 kmph and 360 kmph. Meanwhile, back at the ranch … the fastest train in the U.S.A. is the Amtrak Acela, which tops out at about 257 kmph (160 mph) on special track sections in New Jersey. As anyone who’s ever taken it knows, you ain’t going that fast for most of the trip. Not complaining! Glad the Acela exists. Just saying.

California says that, despite a lot of problems, it is still working on a high-speed, nonstop train between Los Angeles and San Francisco. That train supposedly would do about 350 kmph, too.

Japan and China are working on the next generation of high speed trains that could use maglev technology and go even faster.

Sorry, this is just something I got curious about while taking a lot of trains, and that’s all I’ll bore you with for now, haha.

I stayed in the French Concession district of Shanghai, which was created in the mid-1800s when France, Britain, Germany, Russia and Japan were carving up parts of China into spheres of influence. Today the area is a mix of hotels, apartments, little retail and food/drink shops, parks, big buildings, fancy big-name brand stores, bigger restaurants, mass transit, etc. Shanghai has the highest number of foreign residents of any city in China, so it feels pretty international in places.

And I went to the Propaganda Poster Art Centre, which was a really interesting place. The posters were all created in China to address internal or external governmental priorities. They mostly dated from after the Chinese Communist Party’s 1949 victory in the country’s civil war, although there was a little bit of stuff from the Republic of China period (1912–1949). The art mostly addressed efforts at modernizing the country, and framed relationships with other countries (like the USA) and economic systems. My photo captions are not translations of the Chinese text.
