    Cambodia November 10–16, 2026

    Siem Reap & Stung Treng

    The Khmer empire for centuries was centered in Angkor, on the northern shores of Tonlé Sap Lake (which is part of the Mekong river system). After the last king there decided to relocate to Phnom Penh, the site, which has been called “the most extensive urban complex of the preindustrial world” and was home to an estimated 1 million people, became mostly abandoned and eventually reclaimed by Mother Nature.

    When the French arrived in the 1860s, they cleared the temple of Angkor Wat and a few other sites, but there are more than 200 temples and major buildings that comprise the city, so there are still lots of untouched ruins or ones you can visit but are overgrown with trees and vines and stuff.

    One of the most touristy, but most impressive things you can do at Angkor is visit the main building at sunrise. Siem Reap is the biggest city close to there, so most people get tours that leave at like, 4:30 a.m. in order to get there before the sun comes up. It’s totally worth it.

    Cambodia suffers from a huge amount of land mines that were placed in the ground from the 1970s through the 1990s in its various internal conflicts as well as conflicts with Thailand and Vietnam. While I was in Siem Reap, I visited a site operated by a mine-clearing non-governmental organization known by its Dutch acronym APOPO. The interesting thing about APOPO is they train southern giant pouched rats to recognize smells — including those of explosives inside land mines, tuberculosis and illegally trafficked animals — and help human handlers identify the exact location of the mine.

    At this site, you can see a demonstration of how one of these “hero rats” does its work and even hold one. They’re very big!

    And one of the last days I was there I took a tour through a part of Tonlé Sap, also known as Cambodia’s Great Lake, where people live. They build houses on tall stilts (which is very common throughout Cambodia) and thus use boats during the rainy season when the water in the lake is deep, and can climb down and walk on land when the rain stops and the lake dries up.

    My next big destination was Banlung in northeastern Cambodia, but first I stopped in Stung Treng, which is at the confluence of the Mekong and Sekong rivers. It’s a smaller town and not much is going on, but it is famous for being the birthplace of Sinn Sisamouth, a 1960s singer who is believed to have been killed by the Khmer Rouge. He was a big name in his time, and his (and others’) music has been remembered in the excellent compilation Cambodian Rocks and the documentary Don’t Think I’ve Forgotten.
