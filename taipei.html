Taiwan August 21–28, 2026

Taipei

When I went to Vietnam in 2015, I traveled on a Taiwanese airline and sat next to this Taiwanese guy who really talked up Taiwan, aka the Republic of China, as a great place to visit. That always stuck in my head so I was excited to get to go there.

And lucky for me, when I was in Lijiang, China I met this really nice couple — he is Taiwanese and she is mainland Chinese — who live in Taiwan and wanted to show me around. So basically I got to travel with some friends for a bit on their home turf, which was awesome. Here is the view from my hostel looking out onto the Dongmen Market.

And a video walking through it:

I was in Taipei for about a week and spent the time reading my Taiwan history book and going around seeing sites and eating food. The first thing I learned is that Taiwan is the home to Austronesian people who eventually settled in many more places from Africa to Polynesia. Parts of Taiwan were colonized by Spain and Holland during the mid-1600s, then mainland Chinese, led by Koxinga aka Zheng Chenggong, forced out the Dutch and many more people from southern China migrated there.

Of course, what I mostly knew about Taiwan before I went was that it’s where the nationalists fled when the communists defeated them in 1949, and the Chinese Communist Party considers it part of China and wants to bring it under its control. And that the USA’s commitment to protect Taiwan has evolved over the years.

The majority of people now in Taiwan are Han Chinese, and they speak and write traditional Chinese, in contrast to the People’s Republic of China, where the CCP simplified written Chinese. So if you’re using a translation app, you have to switch to “traditional” Chinese if you go to Taiwan. The people on Taiwan also didn’t go through the Great Leap Forward or Cultural Revolution, so there are some cultural differences from the mainland.

When I was there, it was the Ghost Festival, a Daoist-Buddhist tradition where you put food and incense on a table outside for ghosts who come out of the underworld for one lunar month. The idea is that the ghosts that are roaming around either don’t have descendants to pay homage to them or have descendants who’ve forgotten or neglected them, so everyone pitches in to help feed and entertain those spirits.

Modern Taiwan was also shaped by the Japanese, who occupied it from 1895 to 1945 and developed infrastructure like the national railways and other things, then the nationalists who arrived and took over. The leader of the nationalists at that time was Chiang Kai Shek, and they spared no expense in honoring him after he died. I went to his monument in Taipei, which resembles but is quite a bit larger than the Temple of Heaven in Beijing.

But his dictatorial reign, facilitated by his Kuomintang Party (which still exists), is also remembered as an oppressive period when free speech and criticism of the government was not tolerated. So, in the memorial building, there’s a big section dedicated to Taiwanese people’s fight to win free speech and a democratic government. It makes for an interesting contrast, to walk across the hall from the hagiography of the Chiang Kai Shek section to where they recreated the burned out office of Cheng Nan-jung, a newspaper publisher who lit himself on fire in his newsroom rather than be arrested for “insurrection” related to his publication of a proposed new Taiwanese constitution.

The KMT was in charge of the government in mainland China since the 1920s, so when the communists ousted them in 1949, they fled to Taiwan with massive amounts of gold, silver and cash from China and most of the most culturally valuable artifacts from the Palace Museum, among other things.

You can go to the Palace Museum in Beijing, it’s in the Forbidden City complex, but Taipei has its own Palace Museum with all the good stuff. Here is a bit of what was on display when I was there. It’s a really big museum, you could spend all day there and exhaust yourself.

Night markets are a big thing in Taiwan, so I went to three different ones in Taipei. They vary in size and selection but basically there are food stalls with all kinds of different stuff, from scallion-egg pancakes to these peanut ice cream roll things they make with cilantro (so good). One of my favorite things I found there was bitter tea, this very bitter herbal concoction you are supposed to drink after you eat all that hot, greasy street food. It’s supposed to cool you down, and it definitely works… And the markets usually have a section with carnival-type games, like shoot the balloon, throw the ring around the bottle, etc. And the markets generally happen every night.

One day I went to the Taipei 101 building, which is a big skyscraper with an observation deck. The coolest thing about this place was the Tuned Mass Damper that’s open to the public. It’s a giant metal ball that helps reduce sway in the building in high winds or earthquakes.
